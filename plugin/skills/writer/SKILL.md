# Write for the requested reader
Use the user's purpose, audience, length, and supplied material. Draft directly when these are clear; propose an outline only when it resolves a substantive structure decision.
Lead with the finding, decision, or ask and give enough context to understand it. Use literal descriptive titles, connected paragraphs, concrete verbs, and lists or tables only when they help.
Preserve manual edits and intended meaning. Keep uncertainty when evidence is uncertain. Do not invent measurements, quotations, owners, dates, commitments, notifications sent, or stakeholder agreement.
Distinguish observed results, estimates, assumptions, targets, and proposals. Source material supplies facts; example documents only demonstrate structure. Use labeled placeholders where missing information is necessary.
Match the requested form: a short message can remain a paragraph; a decision memo needs its rationale and tradeoffs. Do not require a fixed sequence of confirmation, outline, full draft, and feedback question for every edit.
If multiple audience versions are requested, derive them from the same evidence and preserve material qualifications in each. When the framing changes, check the whole narrative and companion summaries, including the intended outcome, success measures and next decision; do not repair only the sentence that prompted feedback.
For a formal brief, planning template, presentation, or formatted document, use its applicable workflow instead of imposing a competing template.
Return the usable text. Send or publish it only when authorized.

Use the agreed actor/customer vocabulary consistently. Do not invent a new persona label for each task or replace a clear role with a broader ambiguous one. Shortening must preserve material conditions, exclusions and acceptance detail; restore detail when a concise statement becomes misleading.

## Connected explanatory product writing

Unless the requested audience or format calls for another style, use professional, conversational prose that connects the user's situation, the purpose of the work and the intended outcome. Explain the meaning behind facts rather than listing disconnected assertions.

Preserve the central user outcome throughout the explanation. Setup, discovery or engineering obstacles may explain what gets in the way, but should not silently replace the actual product problem. Describe expected value as a benefit to the user or business rather than a list of tasks. Introduce the proposed product and unfamiliar concepts before detailing its workflow, roles or implementation.

Make clear whether each statement explains, supports, qualifies or contrasts with the preceding point. For a product gap, it can help to follow the user's need through existing options and their limitations to the proposed response. Adapt the sequence to the subject. Use transitions such as "however" or "therefore" only when the reasoning supports them; a connective alone cannot repair a missing explanation. Check that conclusions follow from the preceding facts: supply a supported missing premise or narrow the conclusion rather than assume the connection.

Define ambiguous terms through concrete behavior on first use: who acts, what prompts the action, which system decides what happens next and what the user may experience. Verify that meaning against the sources instead of inventing precision. Distinguish configuration, actions, intermediate decisions and observed outcomes; a count of configured features does not establish usage or effectiveness.

Explain what an initiative or other named effort seeks to achieve and how the proposal could contribute. Preserve supported designations and distinguish a possible application from an agreed dependency or delivery commitment.

For example, in a fictional research tool: "A researcher who repeats a search may benefit from saving it. Opening the search page does not tell us whether the researcher is repeating an investigation or starting a new one, so page visits alone are a weak basis for that suggestion. A proposed rule could use repeated searches to identify a more suitable moment, then test whether the suggestion helps researchers return to useful results." This illustrates the reasoning, not a validated requirement or result.

## Editorial review

Use these checks flexibly rather than imposing a document formula:

- Give each paragraph one main job and ensure it advances the argument.
- Explain the practical consequence of a limitation for the affected person, such as extra work, uncertainty or a less useful experience.
- Keep the actor visible when responsibility matters: say who configures, decides, reviews or acts.
- Carry a consistent example through an explanation when it makes alternatives easier to compare.
- Distinguish prototypes, proposed integrations, Beta capabilities and verified production behavior through clear tense and labels. Deployment alone does not establish readiness or validated benefit.
- Place evidence beside the claim it supports and explain its relevance. Prevalence is not proof of cause or impact.
- Remove repeated explanations while retaining context needed by readers entering a section independently. Keep qualifications close to the relevant claims without repeating them unnecessarily.
- Check the reader's likely next question after each paragraph and use it to guide the sequence.

Keep the result concise, with literal headings and lists where information is parallel or sequential. Add the connections needed for understanding, not padding; a one-pager should remain a concise introduction to its full brief.
