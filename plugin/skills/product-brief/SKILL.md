# Draft a product brief
Read the provided evidence, constraints, existing draft, and requested template. Infer known context before asking questions. Label assumptions rather than inventing user demand, metrics, delivery dates, or dependencies.
For a full brief, the default structure is Problem Statement, Proposed Solution, User Value, Success Metrics, Scope, Open Questions, Dependencies, and Timeline. Adapt or omit sections for a short brief or a supplied template; headings are not evidence of quality.
Define the affected users and problem before the proposed solution. Include the alternative of improving an existing capability when relevant.
Separate prototypes, supported capabilities, proposed integrations and verified production behavior; deployment alone does not establish readiness or validated benefit. State scope exclusions with a reason or revisit condition where useful.
For metrics, name the outcome, population, denominator, window, baseline source, and proposed target. Mark unavailable baselines as unknown and targets as proposals. Avoid assigning a numerical target solely to fill a table.
Separate dependencies discovered in evidence from commitments confirmed by their owners. Distinguish suggested dates from agreed dates.
For uncertain proposals, identify the smallest useful discovery step, expected signal, and decision it would support.
Preserve the user's chosen template and wording where possible. Return the draft with material unresolved questions; do not silently create tickets or publish it.

## Explain the product argument

Connect the affected person's need to the available options, what each leaves unresolved and how the proposal could help. Make the relationship between statements explicit—explanation, evidence, contrast, qualification or consequence—without forcing that progression onto every brief. Check that a claimed product gap follows from the evidence; explain any supported missing premise or narrow the claim. Explain practical consequences before moving into implementation detail.

Check that the sections fulfill their purpose regardless of their headings: the problem explains an unmet user need, the opportunity explains why addressing it matters, the solution introduces what is proposed and the value explains who benefits and how. Setup, discovery and engineering constraints may support that argument, but should not replace the central product problem or turn benefits into implementation tasks. Explain the product and new concepts before the workflow and responsibilities.

Define ambiguous product terms through concrete behavior on first use: who configures or acts, under what conditions, which system makes the next decision and what the user experiences. Distinguish configuration and intermediate actions from observed outcomes; descriptive counts alone do not show cause or effectiveness.

Use a consistent example where it helps explain the alternatives and proposed experience. For a named initiative, explain its intended outcome and the proposal's supported connection to it; do not imply a delivery commitment from a related use case. When framing changes, check the complete brief and companion summary, including the intended outcome, success measures and next decision.

Before finalizing, review paragraph purpose, practical consequences, visible actors, example consistency, current versus proposed behavior, evidence placement, repetition and the reader's likely next question. Treat these as editorial checks, not a fixed template. Preserve literal headings, concise prose and lists where the information is genuinely parallel or sequential.

Keep the main brief focused on the product decision. Consolidate repeated caveats, retain qualifications that change interpretation and link detailed research or operational material instead of turning the brief into an archive.
